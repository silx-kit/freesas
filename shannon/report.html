Evaluation note · FreeSAS

Determining Dmax by Shannon sampling: the formalism is sound, the estimator is not

Evaluation of the method of De Caro et al. (2026) against the dense neural network already in FreeSAS, on 5416 experimental BM29 curves with two independent references (BIFT and GNOM/ATSAS), plus 517 synthetic curves with exact ground truth, apoferritin SASDFN8 and the three test datasets shipped with the repository.

J. Appl. Cryst. 59, 1170–1183 freesas 2026.3.0 · ATSAS 4.1.4 python 3.13 · numpy 2.5.2 2 September 2026

Bottom line

The published method, exactly as specified, does not improve on Dmax = 3·Rg — the heuristic already inside auto_bift. On the least-biased experimental judge the two tie (11.4 % against 11.0 %) and Shannon wins on only 50.6 % of curves; on the synthetic bench, 21 % against 17 %.

The cause is structural, not numerical. The residuals f₁ and f₂ have no minimum: they have a plateau. Past the true Dmax, the Shannon sums recover the same moments. Taking the argmin of a plateau is ill-posed, and the [2.5, 3.5]·Rg search window does all the remaining work — a window that excludes 48 % of the real curves (§6.5).

The most important result is elsewhere, and it reframes the whole question. Across 5416 experimental curves, BIFT and GNOM agree on Rg to 5 % and contradict each other on Dmax by 22 % (§6.1). The implementations are not at fault — freesas and ATSAS return the same Rg to 1.3 %. It is Dmax itself that is poorly constrained by SAXS data. Chasing a 5 %-accurate Dmax estimator makes little sense when the two reference methods do not agree to better than 22 %.

I have withdrawn my plateau-onset variant. It is the worst of the four on experimental data (§6.6): its bias grows with object size, from −17 % to −64 %, so no constant calibration can fix it. The plateau mechanism itself stands; my way of detecting its onset does not survive real data.

The network does not estimate Dmax — it estimates BIFT's Dmax, bias included (§6.2, verified arithmetically). Read it as a fast surrogate for BIFT, not an independent measurement. Its solid structural advantage lies elsewhere: it needs no Guinier fit, and dominates where no usable Guinier region exists (12.5 % against 47 % for Shannon, §6.4).

One advantage of the Shannon route that I had not anticipated: the formalism is unit-covariant — Dmax comes out in the reciprocal of whatever unit q is in — whereas the network is bound to a grid fixed in nm⁻¹ and returns a wrong answer silently when the unit is wrong. Given that SASBDB serves both conventions, this favours Shannon structurally (§3.1).

A hard limit worth knowing: the method requires the first Shannon channel to be measured, i.e. Dmax ≤ π/q_min — 36.9 nm on this instrument (§6.3). Beyond that it loses the dominant term of its alternating series and degrades abruptly.

2. Why the published method fails

2.1 The residuals have a plateau, not a minimum

The method looks for the Dmax at which the moments reconstructed on the Shannon channels match those of the Guinier plot. Measured on apoferritin SASDFN8 with nmax = 11, Rg_NS(Dmax) saturates.

This is physically expected: under-estimating Dmax aliases the alternating series and corrupts the moments, whereas over-estimating it is harmless — extra channels are added and the series still converges to the same moments. The practical consequence is brutal: on a noise-free synthetic sphere, a +0.07 % shift in Rg moves the estimate by 21 %.

2.2 Equation 12 has no diagnostic power

The paper's self-consistency criterion — the two minima must agree to within 1 %, presented as a validation of the Guinier plot — is satisfied in 76 % of the 455 cases, and the median error is the same whether it holds or not: 18.26 % against 18.60 %. Once the plateau is understood this is unsurprising: the criterion compares two near-random positions on a plateau, and their occasional coincidence proves nothing.

The estimate stays glued to the centre of the window whatever the truth is. The [2.5, 3.5]·Rg window is centred on 3·Rg — hence the tie with the control. Note that the paper itself allows “other suitable intervals, if needed”, and that its published Dmax for apoferritin (126.4 Å = 2.42·Rg) falls outside the window it states.

2.4 This is not an implementation defect

Checked before concluding:

  • Equations 3 and 5 are correct. At the exact Dmax and on a fine grid, I(0) and Rg are recovered to within 0.01–0.5 % for all four shapes tested.
  • The truncation corrections (eq. 10–11) work. On the parabolic case at nmax = 5, the error on I(0) drops from 1.95 % to 0.41 %.
  • On apoferritin at the published Dmax, the sums give I0 to −1.3 % and Rg to −2.4 % — and those −2.4 % are exactly the gap between my Guinier Rg and theirs.
  • All free parameters were swept (sensitivity.py): nmax from 4 to 20, mean-filter width on f₁/f₂ from 1 to 401 points, the q-adaptive filter parameters (m, α, q̄₀), and the window width. The best setting found (3.9 % median) turned out to be precisely the one that makes the estimator track the window — see §2.3.

3. What the evaluation reveals about the neural network

The network holds up well — 8.75 % median error, 0.23 ms per call, and remarkable stability: on a 10 nm sphere it returns 10.52 to 10.79 nm regardless of the q range supplied, from 0.4 to 12 nm⁻¹. It does carry a structural constraint that is not declared anywhere.

3.1 The network is tied to an absolute scale; the Shannon route is not

freesas.dnn.preprocess() interpolates onto a fixed 1024-point grid over [0, 4] nm⁻¹, with left=1 and right=0. It therefore needs a correct absolute scale on input. The Shannon formalism is covariant: Dmax comes out in the reciprocal of the unit of q, whatever that unit is.

This shows up directly on SASDFX7.dat, whose own header declares REMARK 265 q : defined in inverse Angstroms — a remark that load_scattering_data() does not read.

The last two columns change by exactly a factor of ten (61.01 → 6.10; 71.57 → 7.10): they are merely changing unit. The network goes from 8.52 to 5.66, with no factor of ten — its answer on the mis-scaled input is simply wrong, and nothing flags it. An instructive coincidence: with the wrong unit its Rg (2.541) landed closer to the true value (2.283 nm) than with the right one (1.975).

Faced with SASBDB's mixed units, this is a clear advantage for the Shannon route and a real risk for the network.

3.3 Detecting the unit: what works and what cannot

SASBDB serves both conventions and files rarely say which. No criterion based on the shape of the curve can settle it: under q → 10q every dimensionless product is invariant — q·Rg, q·Dmax, the oversampling ratio, the number of Shannon channels. An absolute scale has to come from elsewhere. Two sources were tested in unit_detect.py.

Unit detection accuracy

The network cross-check — compare its Rg with the unit-covariant Guinier Rg under both hypotheses — does work, but fails exactly where the network leaves its domain (the 40 nm sphere), and its decision margin is not usable: 1.0× to 1.2× on the failures, but also 1.2× on a correct call. It adds nothing over the plain magnitude threshold.

Ambiguity band, assuming credible ranges of 0.08–1.2 Å⁻¹ and 0.8–12 nm⁻¹

The uncertain band is narrow. Recommended resolution order:

  1. Read the header when it declares the unit (SASBDB's REMARK 265 q : defined in inverse Angstroms) — authoritative, and currently ignored by sasio.py.
  2. Otherwise apply the q_max threshold.
  3. Inside the ambiguous band, refuse to guess and require -u/--unit, which the CLIs already provide.

The four datasets examined support step 1: only SASDFX7.dat carries the declaration; SASDFN8.dat and SASDF52.dat have no usable header.

4. A direction that seemed to work: find the plateau onset

Section withdrawn. The measurements below are correct on analytical shapes, but the estimator fails on the 5416 experimental curves — it is the worst of the four against every one of the three references. See §6.6 for the diagnosis. The section is kept because the plateau mechanism it describes remains valid, and because the gap between its synthetic and experimental verdicts is itself informative.

The plateau is not a defect of the data but a physical property (§2.1). The information about Dmax therefore does not sit in the minimum of the residual but in the abscissa at which it drops to the noise floor. That quantity does not depend on the absolute level of the residual — which is to say, not on the accuracy of the Guinier anchor, exactly what ruins the published method.

The onset estimator carries a systematic −15 % bias, corrected by a multiplicative constant of 1.1495 fitted on a first set of 48 cases, then frozen and evaluated on the 52 independent cases of §1. A systematic bias can be calibrated; the chaos of the argmin cannot.

Caveats, to be resolved before any integration

  • The tail is still bad: p90 at 26 %, maximum at 55 %. The worst cases are strongly elongated objects (prolate spheroid ε = 3), where the onset blurs and the estimate falls short.
  • The calibration constant is empirical and was fitted on analytical shapes. It needs validation on real experimental data with a trusted reference Dmax.
  • The onset detection threshold (factor = 1.1) is a free parameter. A threshold-free definition — intersection of the two asymptotes, or maximum of curvature — would be cleaner and remains to be tried.
  • None of these measurements covers low signal-to-noise laboratory data, which is precisely the case the paper puts forward.

6. Validation on 5416 experimental BM29 curves

Sections 1 to 5 rest on analytical shapes. This section confronts them with sandbox/2025/: 5416 experimental curves from the same instrument, q fixed from 0.085 to 4.96 nm⁻¹ over 1000 points, recorded after the network's training set — so a genuinely blind test for it, which the synthetic bench could not provide.

Two independent references: BIFT, the *.out files shipped with the data by free_bift; and GNOM, produced for this evaluation with ATSAS 4.1.4 (autorg → datgnom -r Rg) into sandbox/2025/atsas/*.out. 4115 of 5469 curves succeed (75.2 %); the 1354 failures are autorg rejections (“No Rg found”, “Data quality too low”).

Methodological caveat: the 5416 curves are not 5416 independent measurements — there are 2478 distinct sample prefixes, some represented up to 210 times.

6.3 A hard limit of the method: Dmax ≤ π/q_min

The first Shannon channel sits at q₁ = π/Dmax. If q₁ < q_min it is not measured — and it is the dominant term of the alternating series. On this instrument q_min = 0.0852 nm⁻¹, so the limit is π/q_min = 36.9 nm.

This is a property of the measurement, not of the implementation: no setting works around it. 84 % of this dataset falls inside the valid domain. The network is unaffected — it even improves with one channel missing. Practical corollary: the Shannon method requires q_min ≤ π/Dmax, to be checked before any use and documented if it is ever implemented.

6.5 The search window excludes half the real data

Across the usable curves, Dmax/Rg spans 2.11 to 4.53 (p5–p95), and only 51.8 % fall inside the [2.5, 3.5]·Rg window the paper prescribes. Outside it, the method degrades as predicted in §2.3: 25.08 % for Dmax/Rg > 3.5 against 15.66 % inside. The criticism raised on synthetic data therefore holds in production, at scale.

6.6 Withdrawal: the plateau-onset estimator does not transfer

My §4 proposal fails on real data and I withdraw it. It is the worst of the four estimators against each of the three references (15.99 % vs GNOM, 28.80 % vs BIFT, 18.84 % on the consensus subset) and beats the control on only 27.4 % of curves. The reason is diagnosed: its bias is not a constant but grows with object size.

No multiplicative constant can correct a size-dependent bias; even re-centred on its experimental median it retains 25.7 % scatter. The ×1.1495 calibration fitted in §4 on analytical shapes was a laboratory artefact: my synthetic noise model was too clean, and the real residual floor — higher and structured — trips the onset detection too early. The plateau mechanism (§2.1) remains exact; it is my way of detecting its onset that does not survive the data.

6.7 What real data also corrects in §3.2

The “network domain limit above 40 nm”, measured on synthetic spheres, does not exist on real data: in the Dmax > 40 nm bin the network is the best of the four (10.04 % median deviation, 49.7 % of curves within 10 %). My monodisperse sharp-edged 60–100 nm spheres are outside its training distribution and physically unrepresentative of BioSAXS; real large objects are flexible and polydisperse, with smooth curves the network knows well.

The small-object defect, by contrast, is confirmed and worse: across the 476 curves with Dmax < 5 nm the network returns a median of 7.48 nm against BIFT's 3.46 nm. It does not go below its training range — a floor, not noise. The reference is not at fault: in that bin χ² is 0.98 and σ(Dmax)/Dmax is 3.1 %, comparable to the other bins.

Methodological lesson: the synthetic bench gives ground truth but with unrepresentative shapes; the experimental set gives representative shapes but no ground truth. Both were needed, and the gap between their verdicts is itself information.

7. Recommendation

  1. Do not integrate the method as published. On the least-biased experimental judge it ties with 3·Rg, already in place, and beats it on 50.6 % of curves — a coin flip — for 18 ms per curve.
  2. Handle the unit question upstream, in sasio.py: read the SASBDB unit declaration when present, otherwise apply the q_max threshold, and refuse to guess inside the 0.8–1.2 band by requiring -u/--unit. This is the best value-for-effort fix in the whole evaluation: it benefits the entire chain, not just Dmax.
  3. Fix dnn.py: the small-object floor (it does not go below ~7 nm, §6.7), validation of the sign and order of magnitude of the output, and a q-range check. Also document what the network actually predicts — BIFT's Dmax, bias included (§6.2) — so that nobody mistakes it for an independent measurement.
  4. Drop my plateau-onset variant. It does not transfer to real data (§6.6). To pursue the plateau idea, the onset detection must not depend on a threshold relative to a residual floor — the real floor is too high and too structured.
  5. Do not expect a decisive speed-up in BIFT. 1.1× from plain seeding, 5.9× only at the price of a frozen Dmax. The interesting lever is robustness against local optima, which needs quantifying.
  6. Reconsider the question itself. BIFT and GNOM contradict each other on Dmax by 22 % while agreeing on Rg to 5 %. Before investing in a fast Dmax estimator, it would be worth deciding which Dmax is to be estimated, and documenting that the quantity itself is not determined to better than ~20 % by this kind of data. An honest confidence interval would probably serve users better than a faster point value.

The Shannon formalism remains a real contribution of the paper: equations 3 and 5 recover I(0) and Rg to within 0.01–0.5 % at the correct Dmax, and the truncation corrections cut the error fivefold at low nmax. It is the use made of it to locate Dmax that is ill-posed, not the formalism — and the difficulty lies less in the method than in the quantity being targeted.
